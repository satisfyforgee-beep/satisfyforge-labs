## Fact base

High-confidence facts cross-checked against Boston Public Library and Smithsonian:
- January 15, 1919, Boston's North End.
- A storage tank containing more than 2.3 million gallons of molasses failed shortly after noon.
- Contemporary/retrospective estimates describe a wave up to about 15 feet high and roughly 35 mph at its fastest.
- 21 people died and about 150 were injured.
- Rescue was made harder by the sticky material and debris; cleanup took weeks.
- The tank owner tried to blame sabotage/anarchists, but the court-appointed investigation found structural weakness and the company was held liable.
- Later engineering/historical work has continued to examine inadequate construction/design and temperature/viscosity effects.

Research references:
- Boston Public Library: https://www.bpl.org/blogs/post/bostons-great-molasses-flood/
- Smithsonian history: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/without-warning-molasses-january-surged-over-boston-180971251/
- Smithsonian science follow-up: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/why-boston-molasses-disaster-was-so-deadly-180961209/

Do not present the folk claim that Boston still smells like molasses on hot days as verified present-day fact.

### Hook

### Narration
> At lunchtime in Boston, a tank burst—and a 15-foot wall of molasses moved through the North End at an estimated 35 miles an hour.
>
> It was January 15, 1919. A giant storage tank near Commercial Street held more than 2.3 million gallons of molasses used for alcohol production.
>
> Shortly after noon, the tank came apart. Witnesses heard rivets snapping and then saw a brown wave tearing through the neighborhood.
>
> Buildings were damaged. Wagons and debris were swept away. The elevated railway structure was hit. And once the first violent surge slowed, the molasses became another problem entirely: people and rescuers had to fight through an incredibly sticky mess.
>
> Twenty-one people died. Around 150 were injured. Cleanup took weeks.
>
> Then came the blame game. The company suggested anarchists had bombed the tank.
>
> But after years of litigation, the evidence pointed back at the tank itself—structural weakness and inadequate safety. The company was held liable.
>
> So one of Boston's strangest disasters wasn't a myth, or even really about molasses being slow.
>
> It was an engineering failure holding millions of gallons above a crowded neighborhood.
